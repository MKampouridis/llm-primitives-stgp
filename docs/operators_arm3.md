## information theory (session a01_information_theory_rerun2)

### `jensen_shannon_distance`

`jensen_shannon_distance(Hist, Hist) -> Real`

Jensen-Shannon distance (square root of the JS divergence, in bits) between two binned return distributions, e.g. the recent window versus the preceding one: 0 = same shape, 1 = disjoint support; a detector of distributional regime change.

**Definition.** For probability vectors P_t and Q_t on the same 16 bins (rows of the two Hist inputs, renormalised to sum to one), let M = (P + Q)/2 and JSD(P||Q) = 1/2 KL(P||M) + 1/2 KL(Q||M), with KL(A||B) = sum_k A_k log2(A_k / B_k) and 0 log 0 = 0. JSD lies in [0, 1] bits, is symmetric and always finite (no smoothing needed even with empty bins). The output is the Jensen-Shannon distance D_t = sqrt(JSD(P_t||Q_t)), a true metric on distributions. It is computed bar by bar; NaN if either row is missing. Typical use: P = rolling_hist(ret, w), Q = past_hist(ret, w), measuring how much information distinguishes the current return distribution from the previous one.

**References.**
- Lin, J. (1991). Divergence measures based on the Shannon entropy. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 37(1), 145-151.
- Endres, D. M. and Schindelin, J. E. (2003). A new metric for probability distributions. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 49(7), 1858-1860.
- Cover, T. M. and Thomas, J. A. (2006). Elements of Information Theory, 2nd ed. Wiley.

### `lagged_mutual_information`

`lagged_mutual_information(Series, window: LongWindow, lag: Lag) -> Real`

Bias-corrected mutual information between the series and its own value `lag` bars earlier, both coded into window terciles: how much (linear or nonlinear) information the past carries about the present.

**Definition.** In the window [t-w+1, t] code each valid x_i into a symbol s_i in {0, 1, 2} by the empirical terciles of the window's valid values (lower-type quantiles, so thresholds are data values). Over the w - lag pairs (s_{i-lag}, s_i) lying inside the window and both valid (n pairs) form the joint frequencies p_ab and marginals p_a, p_b. The plug-in mutual information I = sum_ab p_ab ln(p_ab / (p_a p_b)) is corrected for its positive finite-sample bias with the Miller-Madow first-order term under independence, I_MM = I - (K_a - 1)(K_b - 1) / (2n) with K_a, K_b the numbers of occupied symbols, and normalised by ln 3 (the maximum), so independence gives values near 0 (possibly slightly negative) and perfect predictability gives 1. Applied to ret it captures serial dependence of signed moves including volatility clustering (extreme terciles following each other); applied to abs_ret it measures volatility persistence. Rank coding makes it scale-invariant. NaN if fewer than 80% of values or pairs are valid.

**References.**
- Shannon, C. E. (1948). A mathematical theory of communication. Bell System Technical Journal 27, 379-423 and 623-656.
- Fraser, A. M. and Swinney, H. L. (1986). Independent coordinates for strange attractors from mutual information. Physical Review A 33(2), 1134-1140.
- Darbellay, G. A. and Wuertz, D. (2000). The entropy as a tool for analysing statistical dependences in financial time series. Physica A 287(3-4), 429-439.
- Paninski, L. (2003). Estimation of entropy and mutual information. Neural Computation 15(6), 1191-1253.

### `lempel_ziv_complexity`

`lempel_ziv_complexity(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Lempel-Ziv match-length estimate of the entropy rate (bits per symbol) of the series binarised at its window median: near 1 when above/below-median sequences are incompressible, lower when they repeat earlier patterns (compressible dynamics).

**Definition.** Take the valid values v_1..v_n of x in the window [t-w+1, t] (NaN output if n < 0.8 w) and binarise them at their median m: b_i = 1 if v_i > m else 0 (this makes the feature invariant to positive rescaling and balances the symbols). For i = 2..n let Lambda_i be the length of the shortest substring b_i..b_{i+L-1} that does not occur starting at any earlier position j < i (overlap allowed, truncated at the end of the string); this is one plus the longest match of the future in the past, the quantity underlying Lempel-Ziv parsing and compression. The output is the increasing-window Lempel-Ziv entropy-rate estimator H_t = [ (1/(n-1)) sum_{i=2}^{n} Lambda_i / log2(i) ]^{-1} bits per symbol, which converges to the entropy rate for stationary ergodic sources (Wyner-Ziv, Ornstein-Weiss). Fair i.i.d. bits give values near 1 (with a finite-sample bias that is the same for every instrument at a given window); persistent, clustered or periodic symbol sequences give lower values. On ret it measures the compressibility of up/down regimes; on abs_ret that of calm/turbulent volatility states.

**References.**
- Lempel, A. and Ziv, J. (1976). On the complexity of finite sequences. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 22(1), 75-81.
- Wyner, A. D. and Ziv, J. (1989). Some asymptotic properties of the entropy of a stationary ergodic data source with applications to data compression. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 35(6), 1250-1258.
- Kontoyiannis, I., Algoet, P. H., Suhov, Yu. M. and Wyner, A. J. (1998). Nonparametric entropy estimation for stationary processes and random fields, with applications to English text. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 44(3), 1319-1327.
- Giglio, R., Matsushita, R., Figueiredo, A., Gleria, I. and Da Silva, S. (2008). Algorithmic complexity theory and the relative efficiency of financial markets. EPL (Europhysics Letters) 84(4), 48005.

### `permutation_entropy`

`permutation_entropy(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Normalised Shannon entropy of order-3 ordinal patterns of the series over a trailing window: 1 = all up/down orderings equally likely, lower = predictable ordinal structure.

**Definition.** For each i let pi_i be the ordinal pattern (permutation that sorts) of the triple (x_{i-2}, x_{i-1}, x_i), one of 3! = 6 patterns (ties are resolved by treating equal values as not greater). Over the window [t-w+1, t] take the w-2 triples lying fully inside it, drop any with a missing value, and form the relative frequencies p_k of the six patterns. The output is the normalised permutation entropy H_t = -sum_k p_k ln p_k / ln 6, in [0, 1]. NaN if fewer than 80% of the w-2 triples are complete. Ordinal patterns depend only on order relations, so the feature is invariant to positive rescaling (indeed to any increasing transform) of the input.

**References.**
- Bandt, C. and Pompe, B. (2002). Permutation entropy: a natural complexity measure for time series. Physical Review Letters 88(17), 174102.
- Zanin, M., Zunino, L., Rosso, O. A. and Papo, D. (2012). Permutation entropy and its main biomedical and econophysics applications: a review. Entropy 14(8), 1553-1577.
- Zunino, L., Zanin, M., Tabak, B. M., Perez, D. G. and Rosso, O. A. (2009). Forbidden patterns, permutation entropy and stock market inefficiency. Physica A 388(14), 2854-2864.

## information geometry (session a02_information_geometry)

### `fisher_rao_gaussian_distance`

`fisher_rao_gaussian_distance(Series, window: Window, baseline: LongWindow) -> Real`

Fisher-Rao (Rao) geodesic distance between the Gaussian fitted to the last `window` values and the Gaussian fitted to the last `baseline` values: how atypical the current local state (location and dispersion jointly) is on the statistical manifold.

**Definition.** The univariate normal family N(mu, sigma) with the Fisher information metric ds^2 = (dmu^2 + 2 dsigma^2) / sigma^2 is, after u = mu/sqrt(2), a hyperbolic half-plane of curvature -1/2. Fit maximum-likelihood (mu_s, sigma_s) on the last w values and (mu_b, sigma_b) on the last B values (NaN-aware, each needing >= 80% valid). The output is the closed-form Rao distance d = sqrt(2) * arccosh(1 + ((mu_s - mu_b)^2 / 2 + (sigma_s - sigma_b)^2) / (2 sigma_s sigma_b)). d = 0 when the local fit equals the baseline; it grows with a mean shift measured in units of the local dispersions and logarithmically with a volatility ratio (pure scale change: d = sqrt(2) |log(sigma_s/sigma_b)|). Invariant to rescaling the input; NaN if either dispersion is zero.

**References.**
- Rao, C. R. (1945). Information and the accuracy attainable in the estimation of statistical parameters. Bulletin of the Calcutta Mathematical Society 37, 81-91.
- Atkinson, C. & Mitchell, A. F. S. (1981). Rao's distance measure. Sankhya: The Indian Journal of Statistics, Series A 43(3), 345-365.
- Costa, S. I. R., Santos, S. A. & Strapasson, J. E. (2015). Fisher information distance: a geometrical reading. Discrete Applied Mathematics 197, 59-69.

### `fisher_rao_mirror_asymmetry`

`fisher_rao_mirror_asymmetry(Hist) -> Real`

Signed Fisher-Rao geodesic distance between the binned return distribution and its reflection about the centre: 0 = symmetric, positive = mass tilted to the upper side, negative = tilted to the lower side (range -pi .. pi).

**Definition.** The reflection R: c -> -c of the (symmetric) robust-z bins acts on the probability simplex as the permutation (Rp)_i = p_{N-1-i}, an isometry of the Fisher information metric whose fixed-point set is the submanifold of symmetric distributions. The Fisher-Rao (geodesic) distance between p and its image is d(p, Rp) = 2 arccos(sum_i sqrt(p_i p_{N-1-i})), which is twice the distance from p along the geodesic to its symmetric midpoint; d = 0 iff p is symmetric and d = pi when p and Rp have disjoint supports. The output is A_t = sign(sum_i c_i p_i) * d(p, Rp): the magnitude is a parametrisation-free, bounded and outlier-robust measure of shape asymmetry, the sign tells whether the distribution leans to the upper (positive) or lower (negative) side of its robust centre. Pointwise in t; NaN rows give NaN.

**References.**
- Bhattacharyya, A. (1943). On a measure of divergence between two statistical populations defined by their probability distributions. Bulletin of the Calcutta Mathematical Society 35, 99-109.
- Kass, R. E. & Vos, P. W. (1997). Geometrical Foundations of Asymptotic Inference. Wiley, New York.
- Amari, S. (2016). Information Geometry and Its Applications. Applied Mathematical Sciences 194, Springer.

### `fisher_rao_path_efficiency`

`fisher_rao_path_efficiency(Series, horizon: LongWindow) -> Real`

Geodesic efficiency of the path traced on the Gaussian statistical manifold by six consecutive block fits over the last `horizon` bars: Fisher-Rao displacement divided by information length (near 1 = the distribution migrates steadily, e.g. a volatility trend; near 0 = it wanders and returns).

**Definition.** Split the last H bars into K = 6 consecutive non-overlapping blocks of b = H/6 bars and fit a Gaussian theta_j = (mu_j, sigma_j) to each by maximum likelihood (NaN-aware; >= 80% of the H bars valid overall). With the Fisher metric ds^2 = (dmu^2 + 2 dsigma^2)/sigma^2 the normal family is a hyperbolic half-plane and the Rao distance is d(theta, theta') = sqrt(2) arccosh(1 + ((mu - mu')^2/2 + (sigma - sigma')^2)/(2 sigma sigma')). The information length of the piecewise-geodesic trajectory is L = sum_{j=1}^{K-1} d(theta_{j-1}, theta_j) (a discretisation of the integral of the Fisher speed), and the output is the geodesic efficiency E = d(theta_0, theta_{K-1}) / L in [0, 1]. By the triangle inequality E <= 1, with equality when the block distributions lie in order on one geodesic: a directed, persistent change of the return distribution (steadily rising or falling volatility, a shift of location); small E means the distribution oscillates around a stable state. Scale-invariant; NaN if a block has fewer than 2 valid values or zero dispersion.

**References.**
- Atkinson, C. & Mitchell, A. F. S. (1981). Rao's distance measure. Sankhya: The Indian Journal of Statistics, Series A 43(3), 345-365.
- Crooks, G. E. (2007). Measuring thermodynamic length. Physical Review Letters 99, 100602.
- Heseltine, J. & Kim, E. (2016). Novel mapping in non-equilibrium stochastic processes. Journal of Physics A: Mathematical and Theoretical 49, 175002.
- Amari, S. & Nagaoka, H. (2000). Methods of Information Geometry. Translations of Mathematical Monographs 191, American Mathematical Society / Oxford University Press.

### `gaussian_projection_divergence`

`gaussian_projection_divergence(Hist) -> Real`

Kullback-Leibler divergence (nats) from the binned return distribution to its m-projection onto the discretised Gaussian exponential family: the information lost by describing the market's current distribution with mean and variance alone.

**Definition.** Let c_i be the 16 bin centres and M = {q_theta(i) = exp(theta_1 c_i + theta_2 c_i^2 - psi(theta))} the two-parameter exponential (e-flat) family of discretised Gaussians on them, psi the log-partition function. For the binned distribution p (a Hist row), the m-projection q* = argmin_{q in M} KL(p || q) is the unique member whose expectation parameters match p: E_q*[c] = E_p[c], E_q*[c^2] = E_p[c^2]; it is found by Newton's method on the convex dual psi(theta) - <theta, eta_p> (Hessian = Fisher information Cov_q(c, c^2)). The output is D_t = KL(p || q*) = sum_i p_i log(p_i / q*_i). By the generalised Pythagorean theorem of the dually flat structure, D = H(q*) - H(p) and KL(p || q) = D + KL(q* || q) for every q in M, so D is the part of the distribution (skew, fat or thin tails, multimodality) orthogonal to the Gaussian manifold: the excess structure beyond the first two moments. 0 for an exactly discretised Gaussian. Pointwise in t; NaN rows give NaN.

**References.**
- Csiszar, I. (1975). I-divergence geometry of probability distributions and minimization problems. Annals of Probability 3(1), 146-158.
- Amari, S. (2001). Information geometry on hierarchy of probability distributions. IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 47(5), 1701-1711.
- Amari, S. & Nagaoka, H. (2000). Methods of Information Geometry. Translations of Mathematical Monographs 191, American Mathematical Society / Oxford University Press.

## optimal transport (session a03_optimal_transport)

### `monotone_rearrangement_index`

`monotone_rearrangement_index(Path, window: Window) -> Real`

Optimality of the time-ordered coupling between the calendar clock and the price occupation measure over the window: +1 when the path is the monotone (Brenier) map, i.e. a non-decreasing path; -1 when it is the anti-monotone one; near 0 for a path that wanders without order.

**Definition.** Over the last w bars let mu be the uniform measure on the clock times s = 1..w and nu = (1/w) sum_s delta_{p_s} the occupation measure of the log price. The path itself is a coupling pi_path = (1/w) sum_s delta_{(s, p_s)} of mu and nu. For the quadratic cost c(s,p) = (s-p)^2, minimising the transport cost is equivalent to maximising the gain G(pi) = sum (s - sbar) p; by Brenier's theorem in one dimension (equivalently the Hardy-Littlewood rearrangement inequality) the optimum is the comonotone coupling, pairing the sorted prices p_(1) <= ... <= p_(w) with s = 1..w, with gain G* = sum_s (s - sbar) p_(s), and the worst coupling (anti-monotone) has gain -G*. The index is I = G(pi_path) / G* in [-1, 1] (0 if the window is flat): I = 1 iff the path is cyclically monotone, i.e. itself the optimal transport map from time to price; I = -1 iff it is the anti-monotone map. Invariant to adding a constant to and to positively rescaling the path.

**References.**
- Villani, C. (2003). Topics in Optimal Transportation. Graduate Studies in Mathematics 58, American Mathematical Society. (Ch. 2: one-dimensional case, monotone rearrangement.)
- Brenier, Y. (1991). Polar factorization and monotone rearrangement of vector-valued functions. Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics 44(4), 375-417.
- Hardy, G. H., Littlewood, J. E. and Polya, G. (1952). Inequalities, 2nd ed., Ch. X (rearrangements). Cambridge University Press.

### `transport_direction`

`transport_direction(Hist, Hist) -> Real`

Net direction of the Wasserstein-1 optimal transport plan from the second distribution (e.g. the preceding window) to the first (the recent window): +1 = every unit of mass moves up (first-order stochastic dominance), -1 = every unit moves down, near 0 = mass spreads or contracts without a net shift.

**Definition.** Let a (target) and b (source) be probability vectors on the 16 fixed bins with centres z_1 < ... < z_16 and cumulative distribution functions F_a, F_b. On the line the monotone (quantile) coupling is optimal for every convex cost and, for the cost |x-y|, its total cost is W1(a,b) = sum_{k=1}^{15} |F_b(k) - F_a(k)| (z_{k+1} - z_k): the mass carried across each cut between bins k and k+1 is |F_b(k) - F_a(k)|, upward when F_b(k) > F_a(k) and downward otherwise. The output is the net share of transport work done upward, D = sum_k (F_b(k) - F_a(k)) dz_k / W1(a,b) in [-1, 1] (0 if a = b). D = +1 iff a first-order stochastically dominates b, D = -1 iff b dominates a; |D| < 1 when the plan moves mass both ways (a change of spread or shape).

**References.**
- Vallender, S. S. (1974). Calculation of the Wasserstein distance between probability distributions on the line. Theory of Probability and Its Applications 18(4), 784-786.
- Santambrogio, F. (2015). Optimal Transport for Applied Mathematicians. Birkhauser, Progress in Nonlinear Differential Equations 87 (Ch. 2: one-dimensional transport).
- Rachev, S. T. and Ruschendorf, L. (1998). Mass Transportation Problems, Vol. I: Theory. Springer.

### `variance_clock_transport`

`variance_clock_transport(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Normalised Wasserstein-1 distance between the realised-variance (business-time) clock and the calendar clock over the window: 0 = variance spread evenly in time, 1 = all of it in a single end bar; high values mark bursts, clusters or trends of volatility.

**Definition.** Over the last w bars s = 1..w define the variance-clock measure mu = sum_s m_s delta_{s/w} with m_s = x_s^2 / sum_r x_r^2 (the quadratic-variation time change of Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz / Clark subordination) and the calendar measure lambda = (1/w) sum_s delta_{s/w}. On the line W1(mu, lambda) = int |F_mu - F_lambda|, here (1/w) sum_{k=1}^{w-1} |sum_{s<=k} m_s - k/w|. Its maximum over probability vectors, (w-1)/(2w), is attained when all mass sits on the first or the last bar, so the output is V = W1(mu, lambda) / ((w-1)/(2w)) in [0, 1] (0 for an all-zero window). V is the earth mover's cost of re-timing calendar time into business time; it is invariant to rescaling x.

**References.**
- Villani, C. (2003). Topics in Optimal Transportation. AMS, Graduate Studies in Mathematics 58 (Thm 2.18: W1 on the line as the L1 distance of distribution functions).
- Vallender, S. S. (1974). Calculation of the Wasserstein distance between probability distributions on the line. Theory of Probability and Its Applications 18(4), 784-786.
- Ane, T. and Geman, H. (2000). Order flow, transaction clock, and normality of asset returns. Journal of Finance 55(5), 2259-2284.

### `wasserstein_shape_discrepancy`

`wasserstein_shape_discrepancy(Series, window: Window, baseline: LongWindow) -> Real`

Quadratic Wasserstein distance between the location-scale-normalised distributions of the last `window` values and of the `baseline` values before them: the pure change of shape (skew, tails, modality) that a mean/variance comparison cannot see (0 = same shape up to location and scale, max 2).

**Definition.** Let A be the last w values and B the b values before them, with empirical quantile functions Q_A, Q_B (Q_A = order statistics of A at levels u_j = (j-1/2)/w; Q_B interpolated linearly at the same levels). On the line W2^2(A,B) = int_0^1 (Q_A(u) - Q_B(u))^2 du, which decomposes as (m_A - m_B)^2 + (s_A - s_B)^2 + 2 s_A s_B (1 - rho), where m, s are the mean and standard deviation of the quantile functions and rho = corr(Q_A(U), Q_B(U)) is the quantile correlation. The last term is location-scale free: the output is D = sqrt(2 (1 - rho)) = W2(A~, B~), the W2 distance between the standardised distributions A~ = (A - m_A)/s_A and B~. D lies in [0, 2]; it is invariant to positive rescaling (and shifting) of x.

**References.**
- Villani, C. (2009). Optimal Transport: Old and New. Springer, Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften 338.
- del Barrio, E., Cuesta-Albertos, J. A., Matran, C. and Rodriguez-Rodriguez, J. M. (1999). Tests of goodness of fit based on the L2-Wasserstein distance. Annals of Statistics 27(4), 1230-1239.
- Bickel, P. J. and Freedman, D. A. (1981). Some asymptotic theory for the bootstrap. Annals of Statistics 9(6), 1196-1217.

## rough path theory (session a04_rough_paths)

### `lead_lag_dispersion_area`

`lead_lag_dispersion_area(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Normalised Levy area of the planar path formed by the cumulative (demeaned) returns and the cumulative (demeaned) absolute deviations: which of price and volatility leads the other. Negative = falls precede volatility bursts or bursts precede rebounds (leverage / fear); positive = rises precede volatility or volatility precedes falls.

**Definition.** On the last n = window values x_1..x_n form a_i = x_i - mean(x) and b_i = |a_i| - mean(|a|), and the two-dimensional stream with cumulative coordinates A_j = sum_{i<=j} a_i (price) and B_j = sum_{i<=j} b_i (excess dispersion clock). The level-2 signature of its piecewise-linear interpolation has antisymmetric part (Levy area) L = 1/2 int (A dB - B dA) = 1/2 sum_j (A_{j-1} b_j - B_{j-1} a_j) = 1/2 sum_{i<j} (a_i b_j - b_i a_j), i.e. n/2 times the sum over all positive lags of the antisymmetrised cross-covariance c_ab(k) - c_ba(k). The area is the standard signature statistic for lead-lag between two streams: it is zero when neither leads and its sign tells which one moves first. Output: L / (n * rms(a) * rms(b)), invariant to rescaling the input, O(1) under independence.

**References.**
- Gyurko, L. G., Lyons, T., Kontkowski, M., Field, J. (2013). Extracting information from the signature of a financial data stream. arXiv:1307.7244.
- Flint, G., Hambly, B., Lyons, T. (2016). Discretely sampled signals and the rough Hoff process. Stochastic Processes and their Applications 126(9), 2593-2614.
- Chevyrev, I., Kormilitzin, A. (2016). A primer on the signature method in machine learning. arXiv:1603.03788.
- Black, F. (1976). Studies of stock price volatility changes. Proceedings of the American Statistical Association, Business and Economic Statistics Section, 177-181.

### `signature_shape_novelty`

`signature_shape_novelty(Path, window: Window) -> Real`

Distance between the depth-4 signatures of the last `window` bars of the time-augmented, volatility-normalised log-price path and of the `window` bars before it: 0 = the market is tracing the same path shape (direction, curvature, ordering of moves) as before, large = the shape of the path has changed.

**Definition.** Split the last 2w increments of the path into the preceding block P and the recent block R (w = window each). Each block is turned into a planar piecewise-linear path Z = (s, Y) with s running uniformly over [0, 1] and Y the cumulative increments divided by the block's own root quadratic variation (so sum dY^2 = 1: shape, not volatility level, is compared). The truncated signature S^{<=4}(Z) = (S^1, ..., S^4), S^k = int_{0<u_1<...<u_k<1} dZ_{u_1} (x) ... (x) dZ_{u_k} in (R^2)^{(x)k}, is computed exactly by Chen's identity S(Z * v) = S(Z) (x) exp(v) over the linear segments v. Output: d = ( sum_{k=1..4} ||S^k(R) - S^k(P)||_2^2 )^{1/2}, the Euclidean distance in truncated tensor algebra. The signature determines a time-augmented path uniquely (Hambly-Lyons), and its levels are graded descriptors: net normalised move (level 1), Levy area/convexity (level 2), and higher-order shape at levels 3-4. Invariant to adding a constant to, or rescaling, the path.

**References.**

### `time_levy_area`

`time_levy_area(Path, window: Window) -> Real`

Levy area of the time-augmented log-price path over the window, in units of the path's quadratic-variation scale: positive when the path lies below its chord (convex: accelerating rise or V-shaped recovery), negative when above it (concave: rounding top, decelerating rise, accelerating fall).

**Definition.** Take the last n = window increments of the path, X_{t-n}, ..., X_t, set Y_k = X_{t-n+k} - X_{t-n} and augment with normalised time s_k = k/n, giving the piecewise-linear planar path Z = (s, Y) on [0, 1]. Its level-2 signature contains the Levy area A = 1/2 (S^{(s,Y)} - S^{(Y,s)}) = 1/2 int_0^1 (s dY_s - Y_s ds), which for a piecewise-linear path equals Y_n/2 - int_0^1 Y_s ds (exact by the trapezoid rule), i.e. the signed area between the chord from (0,0) to (1,Y_n) and the path. Equivalently A = (1/n) sum_m (m - (n+1)/2) dY_m. The output is sqrt(12) * A / sqrt(sum_m dY_m^2): under Brownian motion this is approximately standard normal (Var int_0^1 (B_s - s B_1) ds = 1/12), it is unchanged by adding a constant or rescaling the path, and it vanishes for any straight line, so it carries the curvature (timing of the move) that the level-1 signature (net change) does not.

**References.**
- Levy, P. (1940). Le mouvement brownien plan. American Journal of Mathematics 62(1), 487-550.
- Lyons, T. J., Caruana, M., Levy, T. (2007). Differential Equations Driven by Rough Paths. Ecole d'Ete de Probabilites de Saint-Flour XXXIV, Lecture Notes in Mathematics 1908, Springer.
- Chevyrev, I., Kormilitzin, A. (2016). A primer on the signature method in machine learning. arXiv:1603.03788.

### `two_variation_excess`

`two_variation_excess(Path, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Log of the ratio between the 2-variation of the path over the window (supremum over all partitions of the sum of squared increments) and its quadratic variation on the daily grid: near 0 = choppy, mean-reverting path whose oscillations cancel; large = persistent excursions that coarse partitions capture far better than daily ones.

**Definition.** For the path values X_{t-n}, ..., X_t (n = window) the p-variation with p = 2 is ||X||_{2-var}^2 = sup over partitions t-n = k_0 < k_1 < ... < k_m = t of sum_i |X_{k_i} - X_{k_{i-1}}|^2, computed exactly by the dynamic programme V_j = max_{i<j} (V_i + (X_j - X_i)^2), V_0 = 0. p = 2 is the critical exponent of rough path theory (Young integration below it, the Levy-area lift above it); the finest partition gives the realised quadratic variation QV = sum (dX)^2 <= V_n. Output: log(V_n / QV) >= 0. It is 0 when every coarsening loses variation (strictly alternating increments), grows slowly (log log n) for a random walk, and grows like log(n mu^2 / sigma^2) for a trend; it is invariant to shifting and rescaling the path.

**References.**

- Friz, P. K., Victoir, N. B. (2010). Multidimensional Stochastic Processes as Rough Paths: Theory and Applications. Cambridge University Press (ch. 5, p-variation).

- Taylor, S. J. (1972). Exact asymptotic estimates of Brownian path variation. Duke Mathematical Journal 39(2), 219-241.

## fractal analysis (session a05_fractal)

### `dcca_coefficient`

`dcca_coefficient(Series, Series, window: LongWindow, box: DccaBox) -> Real`

Detrended cross-correlation coefficient rho_DCCA at box scale n between two series over a trailing window (e.g. returns and absolute returns): +1 = their cumulative fluctuations co-move at that time scale, -1 = they move in opposition (e.g. falls accompanied by volatility build-ups), 0 = no scale-n cross-correlation.

**Definition.** Let (x_i, y_i), i = 1..N, be the bars of the last `window` where both series are valid. Profiles R_k = sum_{i<=k} (x_i - mean x), S_k = sum_{i<=k} (y_i - mean y), k = 0..N. For each of the N-n+1 overlapping boxes of n+1 profile points, fit least-squares lines to R and S and let eps^R, eps^S be the residuals. Detrended covariances F^2_xy(n) = sum_boxes mean(eps^R eps^S), F^2_xx(n), F^2_yy(n) likewise. The output is rho_DCCA(n) = F^2_xy(n) / sqrt(F^2_xx(n) F^2_yy(n)) in [-1, 1]. It is the cross-correlation of the two series at time scale n after local trends are removed, the normalised counterpart of the DCCA fluctuation function F_xy(n) ~ n^{lambda} used to detect power-law cross-correlations in non-stationary series. Each series may be rescaled by a positive constant without changing the output.

**References.**
- Podobnik, B., Stanley, H. E. (2008). Detrended cross-correlation analysis: a new method for analyzing two nonstationary time series. Physical Review Letters 100, 084102.

### `dfa_exponent`

`dfa_exponent(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA-1) scaling exponent of the series over a trailing window: 0.5 = no memory, >0.5 = persistent (long-memory, trending) fluctuations, <0.5 = anti-persistent (mean-reverting) fluctuations.

**Definition.** Let x_1..x_N be the valid values in the last `window` bars (missing bars dropped). Profile Y_k = sum_{i<=k} (x_i - mean(x)). For box sizes s on a log-spaced grid of up to 7 values in [4, N/4], take all N-s+1 overlapping (gliding) boxes of s consecutive points of Y, fit a least-squares line in each box and let F(s)^2 be the mean squared residual over all boxes. The output is the DFA exponent alpha = OLS slope of log F(s) on log s. For fractional Gaussian noise alpha = H (Hurst exponent); alpha = 0.5 for uncorrelated increments, alpha > 0.5 long-range persistence, alpha < 0.5 anti-persistence; on absolute returns it measures long memory of volatility. Linear detrending removes the local mean and drift, and F scales linearly with x, so alpha is scale-free.

### `generalized_hurst_spread`

`generalized_hurst_spread(Path, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Degree of multifractality H(1) - H(2) of the log-price path over a trailing window, from the generalized Hurst exponents of its first and second absolute moments: 0 = uniform (monofractal) scaling, positive = large moves scale more slowly than typical ones (intermittent, volatility-clustered dynamics).

**Definition.** Let X_1..X_N be the valid path values in the last `window` bars (missing bars dropped). For lags tau in {1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12} (those <= N/4), the q-th order structure function is K_q(tau) = mean_t |X_{t+tau} - X_t|^q over all overlapping pairs in the window. Under scaling K_q(tau) ~ tau^{q H(q)}, so the generalized Hurst exponent H(q) is the OLS slope of log K_q(tau) on log tau divided by q. The output is Delta H = H(1) - H(2). A monofractal process (e.g. fractional Brownian motion) has H(q) constant and Delta H = 0; multiscaling (multifractal) dynamics, where the largest fluctuations scale differently from the typical ones (volatility clustering, intermittent bursts, slow aggregational convergence of fat tails), give Delta H > 0. Di Matteo et al. relate Delta H to market development: it is larger in less mature, more turbulent markets. Only increments enter (shift-invariant) and the log moments shift by constants under scaling (scale-invariant).

**References.**
- Di Matteo, T., Aste, T., Dacorogna, M. M. (2005). Long-term memories of developed and emerging markets: using the scaling analysis to characterize their stage of development. Journal of Banking & Finance 29(4), 827-851.
- Di Matteo, T. (2007). Multi-scaling in finance. Quantitative Finance 7(1), 21-36.
- Barabasi, A.-L., Vicsek, T. (1991). Multifractality of self-affine fractals. Physical Review A 44(4), 2730-2733.

### `higuchi_dimension`

`higuchi_dimension(Path, window: Window) -> Real`

Higuchi fractal dimension of the log-price curve over a trailing window: near 1 = a smooth, directional path, near 1.5 = a Brownian-like path, near 2 = a rough, space-filling, choppy path.

**Definition.** Let X_1..X_N be the valid path values in the last `window` bars (missing bars dropped). For lags k = 1..kmax with kmax = max(3, min(10, floor(N/5))) and offsets m = 1..k, the normalised length of the subsampled curve X_m, X_{m+k}, X_{m+2k}, ... is L_m(k) = [ sum_{i=1}^{n_m} |X_{m+ik} - X_{m+(i-1)k}| ] * (N-1) / (n_m k) / k, with n_m = floor((N-m)/k). With L(k) = mean_m L_m(k), the curve length scales as L(k) ~ k^{-D}; the output is D = -(OLS slope of log L(k) on log k). D is the fractal (box/curve-length) dimension of the graph: 1 for a smooth trend, 1.5 for Brownian motion, 2 - H for fractional Brownian motion of Hurst index H, toward 2 for a jagged, oscillating path. Only differences enter (shift-invariant) and a scale factor c multiplies every L(k) by c (scale-invariant).

## statistical physics (session a06_statistical_physics)

### `cycle_affinity`

`cycle_affinity(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Schnakenberg cycle affinity of the three-state (window tercile) Markov chain of the series: the thermodynamic force that breaks detailed balance. 0 = time-reversible dynamics; > 0 the cycle low->mid->high->low dominates (gradual build-up, abrupt collapse); < 0 the reverse cycle dominates (abrupt jump, gradual relaxation, e.g. volatility shocks that decay).

**Definition.** Over the trailing window of W bars, code each value into a state sigma_t in {0 (low), 1 (mid), 2 (high)} by the window terciles of x. Count transitions n_ij = #{t : sigma_{t-1} = i, sigma_t = j} over adjacent valid pairs. For a stationary Markov jump process on three states the cycle graph has one independent cycle, and Schnakenberg's network theory gives the total entropy production rate as sigma = J_c * A_c, where J_c is the net probability current around the cycle and A_c = ln[ (P_01 P_12 P_20) / (P_10 P_21 P_02) ] = ln[ (n_01 n_12 n_20) / (n_10 n_21 n_02) ] is the cycle affinity (the stationary occupations cancel in the product). The operator returns A_hat = ln[ prod_forward (n_ij + c) / prod_backward (n_ji + c) ] with c = 1/2. Detailed balance (time-reversal symmetry, equilibrium) holds iff A = 0 (Kolmogorov's criterion); its sign gives the direction of the circulating current and its magnitude the entropy produced per completed cycle (in units of k_B). Computed when at least 80% of the window is valid; invariant to positive rescaling of the series (rank coding).

**References.**
- Schnakenberg, J. (1976). Network theory of microscopic and macroscopic behavior of master equation systems. Reviews of Modern Physics 48(4), 571-585.
- Kolmogorov, A. N. (1936). Zur Theorie der Markoffschen Ketten. Mathematische Annalen 112, 155-160.
- Seifert, U. (2012). Stochastic thermodynamics, fluctuation theorems and molecular machines. Reports on Progress in Physics 75, 126001.
- Roldan, E. and Parrondo, J. M. R. (2010). Estimating dissipation from single stationary trajectories. Physical Review Letters 105, 150607.

### `hawkes_branching_ratio`

`hawkes_branching_ratio(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Branching ratio of a self-exciting (Hawkes) description of extreme moves, from the dispersion of event counts: 0 = shocks arrive independently, -> 1 = the market is near criticality (most shocks are triggered by earlier shocks: reflexive, cascading dynamics), < 0 = shocks are more regular than random.

**Definition.** Over the trailing window of W bars, an event occurs at bar t when the absolute deviation |x_t - median_W(x)| exceeds its own window 80% quantile (the 20% largest moves). In a stationary Hawkes process with branching ratio n = integral of the excitation kernel, each event begets on average n offspring, the total cluster size is 1/(1-n), and the index of dispersion of counts over long intervals is F = Var[N_Delta]/E[N_Delta] -> 1/(1-n)^2 (Hardiman and Bouchaud 2014). With N_k the event counts in all overlapping blocks of Delta = 10 bars inside the window, m and v their mean and variance and p = m/Delta, the dispersion index relative to independent occupation of the window by the same number of events (hypergeometric block counts) is F = v / (m (1 - p) (W - Delta)/(W - 1)), and the operator returns n_hat = 1 - 1/sqrt(max(F, 0.1)). n_hat = 0 for independent events, n_hat -> 1 at the critical (endogenous, cascading) point, and n_hat < 0 for under-dispersed (regular) event sequences. Computed when at least 80% of the window is valid; invariant to positive rescaling (rank-defined events).

**References.**

### `ising_coupling`

`ising_coupling(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Maximum-likelihood nearest-neighbour coupling beta*J of a 1D Ising chain fitted to the series binarised at its window median: > 0 ferromagnetic (states persist: trending signs or clustered volatility), < 0 antiferromagnetic (states alternate), 0 = free spins.

**Definition.** Over the trailing window of W bars, spins s_t = sign(x_t - median_W(x)) in {-1, +1} (missing bars and exact ties are vacant sites, s = 0). The sequence is modelled as a 1D Ising chain with Hamiltonian -beta*H = J * sum_t s_t s_{t+1} + h * sum_t s_t. By the transfer-matrix solution, the stationary pair law is p(s, s') = v(s) T(s, s') v(s') / lambda with T(s, s') = exp(J s s' + h (s + s')/2), so the eigenvector and field factors cancel in the pair odds ratio and p(+,+) p(-,-) / (p(+,-) p(-,+)) = exp(4J) exactly. The (conditional) maximum-likelihood estimate of the coupling is therefore J_hat = (1/4) ln[(n_{++}+c)(n_{--}+c) / ((n_{+-}+c)(n_{-+}+c))], with n_{ab} the counts of adjacent occupied pairs (s_{t-1}, s_t) = (a, b) in the window and c = 1/2 the Haldane-Anscombe correction. The correlation length of the chain is xi = -1/ln tanh|J|. Computed when at least 80% of the window is valid. Invariant to positive rescaling of the series (rank-based spins).

### `spectral_flatness`

`spectral_flatness(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

### `spectral_shape_change`

`spectral_shape_change(Series, window: LongWindow) -> Real`

Gain-free log-spectral distance between the multitaper power spectrum of the last `window` values and that of the `window` values before them: how much the frequency content (the balance of slow and fast fluctuations, i.e. the autocorrelation structure) has changed, irrespective of any change in overall variance. 0 = same spectral shape, larger = the series' dynamics have changed.

**Definition.** Split the last 2N values (N = window) into the preceding block A and the recent block B. For each block (demeaned) compute the sine-multitaper spectrum with K = 5 tapers at the Fourier frequencies j/N, j = 1..floor((N-1)/2), and average it over 8 contiguous equal-width frequency bands: P_b. Normalise to unit total power, p_b = P_b / sum P, and take d_b = log p_b^B - log p_b^A. Output: the gain-free log-spectral distance D = sqrt( (1/8) sum_b (d_b - mean d)^2 ), the standard deviation over bands of the log spectral ratio. A pure change of variance (gain) leaves D unchanged; D measures the change of the spectral shape, i.e. of the normalised autocovariance function. Scale-invariant; NaN if any value in the 2N window is missing.
